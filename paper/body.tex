\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Accurate knowledge of where species occur, and when, is fundamental to conservation biology, ecological research, and biodiversity monitoring. Species distribution models (SDMs) have become indispensable tools for mapping geographic ranges, assessing conservation status, and projecting responses to environmental change \citep{elith2009species,guisan2013predicting,araujo2019standards}. Traditionally, SDMs relate species occurrences to environmental predictor variables using statistical or machine-learning methods, producing habitat-suitability maps for individual species.

The past decade has seen transformative growth in biodiversity observation data, driven largely by citizen-science platforms. eBird, the world's largest biodiversity-related citizen-science project, has accumulated billions of bird observations worldwide \citep{sullivan2009ebird,sullivan2014ebird}. Complemented by broader taxonomic databases such as GBIF and iNaturalist, these datasets now cover tens of thousands of species across all major taxonomic groups. Citizen-science data also pose well-documented challenges: spatial sampling bias concentrated near population centers and road networks, temporal heterogeneity in observer effort, and the absence of true absence records \citep{phillips2009sample,isaac2014statistics,bird2014statistical,kelling2019semistructured,botella2020bias,johnston2021analytical}.

Despite methodological advances in SDM theory and practice, three limitations persist. Classical SDMs such as Maxent, random forests, and boosted regression trees typically model one species at a time, which limits scalability to large taxonomic groups \citep{elith2009species}. Multi-species deep-learning SDMs have emerged as a promising alternative \citep{beery2021sdmreview,botella2018deep,deneu2021convolutional}, but most still require environmental covariates (satellite imagery, climate grids, land-cover maps) at inference time, restricting deployment to regions and time periods where such data are available. Most importantly for our purposes, nearly all of these models produce static range maps: one suitability surface per species, with no temporal dimension. Weekly or seasonal variation in occurrence is either ignored or handled by fitting separate models per time slice, which forfeits the ability to learn shared temporal structure across species.

Recent work on Spatial Implicit Neural Representations (SINRs) shows that neural networks can learn species--location associations directly from coordinates, bypassing the need for environmental inputs at inference \citep{cole2023spatial}. Extensions include language-grounded zero-shot range estimation \citep{hamilton2024combining} and automatic thresholding for binary range maps \citep{dorm2024generating}, while graph-based alternatives encode landscape structure explicitly \citep{harrell2025heterogeneous,wu2025gnn}. Advances in pseudo-absence selection \citep{zbinden2024selection} and observation-bias correction \citep{botella2020bias,johnston2021analytical} have improved the reliability of presence-only SDMs more generally. These models remain spatial only.

Most of this work targets scientific accuracy, maximizing discrimination or calibration for ecological research and conservation planning. A different and complementary use case arises in passive acoustic monitoring. Systems such as BirdNET \citep{kahl2021birdnet} classify thousands of hours of audio into species detections, and they need a spatiotemporal prior to suppress false positives from species that are unlikely at the recording location and time of year \citep{thompson2025post}. Querying eBird or iNaturalist directly would mean shipping hundreds of megabytes of occurrence data and building spatial indices on the client. What is needed instead is a compact model, small enough to bundle with a mobile or embedded application, whose occurrence probabilities are good enough to filter acoustic detections even if they are intentionally coarser than a research-grade SDM. Because such a model learns continuous spatial and temporal patterns rather than memorizing discrete observations, it can also interpolate into areas with sparse survey coverage and smooth out geographic biases in the observation record.

We introduce BirdNET Geomodel, a multi-task neural network built for exactly this purpose. It predicts occurrence probability jointly for over 10,000 species, birds and non-avian taxa, and for each of 48 weeks of the year, from latitude, longitude, and week alone. One set of weights therefore yields 48 occurrence surfaces per species, capturing migration, breeding phenology, and seasonal range contraction without fitting a separate model per species or per time slice. Environmental features enter only as auxiliary regression targets during training. A staged ablation of 46 grid-search experiments and 60 Bayesian optimization trials establishes which design choices matter, and identifies constrained label propagation in environmental space as the dominant one. The trained model fits in approximately 7\,MB as FP16, replacing hundreds of megabytes of raw observation data, and exports to ONNX, TFLite, and SavedModel formats. It is already deployed in BirdNET Analyzer, the BirdNET mobile app, Haikubox, and BirdWeather, where it restricts acoustic detections to locally plausible species.


\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Multi-Species and Deep-Learning SDMs}
\label{sec:related-sdm}

Species distribution models estimate habitat suitability by relating occurrences to environmental or spatial predictors \citep{elith2009species}, under community standards that govern how they should be built, evaluated, and reported \citep{araujo2019standards}, and conservation practitioners rely on them for habitat identification, reserve planning, and translocation assessment \citep{guisan2013predicting}. Classical methods model one species at a time, and ensemble tools such as eSDM \citep{woodman2019esdm} address scalability only partially by combining outputs post hoc.

Deep learning changed that. \citet{beery2021sdmreview} survey the field for machine-learning practitioners and identify presence-only modeling and observation bias as the central challenges. Early deep-learning SDMs applied CNNs to environmental rasters: \citet{botella2018deep} showed that multi-label CNNs capture complex realized niches better than classical methods, \citet{deneu2021convolutional} confirmed that spatial rasters outperform point-based features, and \citet{deneu2022very} pushed resolution to 1\,m remote-sensing imagery. \citet{botella2018species} closed the loop between automated identification and SDMs by training on app-generated plant observations, a precursor to the BirdNET-to-geomodel pipeline described here. Spatial Implicit Neural Representations \citep{cole2023spatial} then estimated ranges for 47,000 species from coordinates alone, approximating expert range maps from noisy citizen-science data; it has since been extended with language grounding \citep{hamilton2024combining} and outlier-robust thresholding \citep{dorm2024generating}. Graph-based models encode species and locations as graph nodes \citep{harrell2025heterogeneous,wu2025gnn}, but rely on explicit environmental inputs at inference. Our architecture inherits SINR's coordinate-only paradigm and adds weekly temporal conditioning through FiLM layers \citep{perez2018film}, plus an auxiliary environmental regression head that shapes the representation without requiring environmental inputs at inference.

Two methodological results shaped our design. \citet{zbinden2024selection} evaluated pseudo-absence selection for deep-learning SDMs and found that loss-function weighting with spatial block cross-validation improves presence-only models. \citet{kellenberger2026performance} compared 2,299 North American species and found that deep-learning models match but do not surpass Maxent and Random Forest on average, and perform substantially worse for range-restricted and threatened species, partly because information leakage across splits inflates deep-learning metrics. Both inform our location-based splitting strategy and per-species watchlist evaluation.

\subsection{Citizen-Science Data and Observation Bias}
\label{sec:related-bias}

Training data for global SDMs come predominantly from citizen-science platforms. eBird \citep{sullivan2009ebird} has scaled to billions of observations through semistructured survey protocols and automated quality control \citep{sullivan2014ebird}, and \citet{kelling2019semistructured} show that effort metadata is essential for robust analysis. \citet{phillips2009sample} showed that target-group background points sharing the collection bias of occurrence data mitigate spatial bias as effectively as the modeling method itself, though \citet{botella2020bias} later showed this correction is unbiased only when collection density is approximately uniform, rarely true globally. \citet{isaac2014statistics} found that simple trend-extraction methods produce biased estimates, \citet{bird2014statistical} showed that mixed-effects and hierarchical frameworks can account for many such errors, and \citet{johnston2021analytical} synthesized analytical guidelines for eBird, identifying complete checklists and effort covariates as the largest performance levers. Our model sees only coordinates and week, so it cannot use effort covariates; it addresses observation bias instead through label propagation between H3 cells and metrics that penalize predictions correlated with observer density (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}).

Standardized benchmarks have organized progress on the multi-species problem. SINR range predictions used as geographic priors improve fine-grained image classification on iNaturalist data \citep{cole2023spatial,vanhorn2021benchmarking} without retraining the visual model, and SatBird pairs satellite imagery with eBird encounter rates, showing that remote-sensing features complement environmental covariates \citep{teng2023satbird}. The GeoLifeCLEF series \citep{botella2025geolifeclef,picek2025lifeclef} moved evaluation to continental-scale species composition, and its 2025 edition \citep{picek2025overview} introduced geographically shifted test sets that expose generalization failure under spatial shift, the failure mode our density-stratified evaluation targets. Downstream, deep-learning SDM features have been used to predict IUCN threat status \citep{estopinan2024extinction}, prioritize conservation action by reinforcement learning \citep{silvestro2022improving}, transfer presence-only models to abundance prediction for rare species \citep{bourel2025abundance}, and classify habitat from species composition \citep{leblanc2024deep}. We extract environmental features through Google Earth Engine, following \citet{crego2022implementation}.

\subsection{eBird Status and Trends, and Acoustic Post-Filtering}
\label{sec:related-ebirdst}

The eBird Status and Trends products model weekly abundance and occurrence for hundreds of species \citep{fink2024ebird,fink2023ebird,fink2022ebird} using Adaptive Spatio-Temporal Exploratory Models \citep{fink2010adastem}, which partition space and time via multiscale ensembles; the ebirdst R package \citep{strimasmackey2023ebirdst} is the reference implementation. They are among the very few SDM products at weekly temporal resolution, and thus the closest available reference for validating our predictions (Section~\ref{sec:res-ebirdst}). Each species is nonetheless modeled independently, the method requires extensive effort covariates, and coverage is strongest in North America. The need for such monitoring is well documented: \citet{rosenberg2019decline} integrated multiple observation networks to document a net loss of roughly 3 billion North American birds over 48 years.

BirdNET \citep{kahl2021birdnet} identifies bird species from audio using a deep ResNet, and like any acoustic classifier it produces false positives for geographically or temporally implausible species. \citet{thompson2025post} address this with a post-processing framework that calibrates species-specific thresholds via generalized linear mixed models. Our approach is complementary: rather than adjusting thresholds after classification, the geomodel supplies a spatiotemporal prior that narrows the candidate list before or during classification.


\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

Our approach rests on two premises. First, latitude, longitude, and week encode enough about climate, habitat, and photoperiod to predict which species are plausible at a location without supplying those covariates explicitly; week is a first-class input rather than a separate model per season. Second, the model must run on mobile phones and embedded acoustic sensors. Environmental features therefore enter only as auxiliary training targets; at inference the model sees nothing but a coordinate and a week.

\subsection{Input Encoding}
\label{sec:encoding}

The model receives raw latitude $\phi \in [-90, 90]$, longitude $\lambda \in [-180, 180]$, and week $w \in \{1, \ldots, 48\}$ as its sole inputs: no environmental covariates, imagery, or species traits. All three are converted to radians and passed through a multi-harmonic circular encoding \citep{tancik2020fourier}:

\begin{equation*}
\text{CE}(\theta; H) = \bigl[\sin(\theta),\; \cos(\theta),\; \sin(2\theta),\; \cos(2\theta),\; \ldots,\; \sin(H\theta),\; \cos(H\theta)\bigr]
\end{equation*}

producing $2H$ features per scalar. The conversions are:

\begin{equation*}
\theta_\phi = \phi \cdot \frac{\pi}{180}, \quad
\theta_\lambda = \lambda \cdot \frac{\pi}{180}, \quad
\theta_w = \frac{2\pi (w - 1)}{48}
\end{equation*}

The week encoding is $2\pi$-periodic, so week 48 wraps smoothly to week 1. We use 48 weeks rather than 52 so that each month holds exactly four, split at days 7, 14, and 21: weeks then stay aligned to months in every year and follow from a date by integer arithmetic. Only the fourth varies, absorbing the month's remainder at 7 to 10 days. We use $H_\text{coord} = 8$ harmonics for latitude and longitude ($2 \times 2 \times 8 = 32$ spatial features) and $H_\text{week} = 8$ for week (16 temporal features). Higher harmonics capture finer spatial and seasonal detail; both came from hyperparameter search (Section~\ref{sec:search}).

\subsection{Model Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

The model is a multi-task network with a shared encoder and two task heads (Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}). A pervasive design constraint is half-precision (FP16) compatibility: it must train stably under automatic mixed precision and export to FP16 for mobile and embedded deployment (Section~\ref{sec:export}), which motivates the bounded FiLM conditioning and conservative LayerNorm epsilon below.

Model capacity is governed by a continuous scale factor. At the reference scale the network has embedding dimension $d = 512$, four encoder blocks, a species-head bottleneck of $b = 128$, and two species-head residual blocks: 7.1 million parameters for 12K species. Halving the scale gives roughly 1.5M parameters; doubling it gives 47M. Dimensions scale linearly, block counts to the nearest integer.

An optional third head routes predicted environmental features through a secondary species classifier, gating its logits against the direct head. It yields no benefit at any scale and is absent from the production model; Appendix~\ref{sec:E-refuted-design-choices} defines it and reports its results.

\subsubsection{Shared Encoder}
\label{sec:encoder}

The concatenated latitude and longitude encodings ($4 H_\text{coord}$ features) are linearly projected to $d$ dimensions and processed through $N$ pre-norm residual blocks:

\begin{equation*}
\mathbf{x} \leftarrow \mathbf{x} + f\bigl(\text{LN}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)
\end{equation*}

where $f$ is a two-layer feed-forward network with LayerNorm, GELU activations, and dropout. The LayerNorm epsilon is set conservatively for stability under FP16 mixed precision.

\textbf{FiLM conditioning.} At each residual block, the week encoding is mapped through a two-layer MLP to produce per-block scale $\gamma$ and shift $\beta$ vectors \citep{perez2018film}:

\begin{equation*}
\gamma = 1 + \tanh(\gamma_\text{raw}), \qquad
\mathbf{x}_\text{out} = \gamma \odot f(\mathbf{x}) + \beta
\end{equation*}

Bounding $\gamma \in (0, 2)$ via $\tanh$ is what makes the architecture FP16-safe: without it, compounding multiplicative scaling through $N$ successive blocks would risk overflow, since FP16 saturates at 65,504. Bounded, the largest configuration tested ($N = 8$) has a worst-case compound amplification of $2^8 = 256$. The FiLM output layers are zero-initialized, so conditioning starts at identity ($\gamma = 1$, $\beta = 0$) and is learned gradually.

\subsubsection{Species Prediction Head}
\label{sec:species-head}

The species head maps the encoder output to $S$ species logits via residual blocks followed by a low-rank bottleneck:

\begin{equation*}
\text{Linear}(d \to d_h) \;\to\; \text{ResBlocks} \;\to\; \text{LN} \;\to\; \text{Linear}(d_h \to b) \;\to\; \text{GELU} \;\to\; \text{Linear}(b \to S)
\end{equation*}

The bottleneck dimension $b \ll S$ reduces the final projection from $d_h \times S$ to $(d_h \times b) + (b \times S)$ parameters, which matters once $S$ exceeds 10,000. The compact bottleneck can also be read as a latent species embedding whose dimensions correspond to ecological niche axes.

\subsubsection{Environmental Prediction Head}
\label{sec:env-head}

The environmental head regresses environmental features (elevation, precipitation, temperature, land-cover fractions, and other bioclimatic variables) from the shared encoder embedding. It is a single residual block followed by a linear projection, serves as an auxiliary training objective only, and is absent at inference. Its purpose is to push the encoder toward representations that capture the physical environment at each location.

\subsection{Data Pipeline}
\label{sec:data}

Training data combines two independent streams: species occurrence records from GBIF and environmental features from Google Earth Engine. The occurrence data is presence-only, recording where a species was seen but not where it was absent, and is heavily biased toward well-surveyed regions. The pipeline therefore aggregates raw observations into a spatially regular grid, fills gaps in under-sampled areas through label propagation, and normalizes species frequencies across regions with vastly different observer effort. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} in Appendix~\ref{sec:G-data-pipeline-diagram} summarizes the sequence.

\subsubsection{Spatial Discretization and Sources}
\label{sec:sources}

The globe is partitioned into hexagonal cells at H3 resolution 4 \citep{uberh3}, yielding approximately 288,000 cells with about 26\,km average edge length. Species occurrence data is ingested from a GBIF Darwin Core Archive bulk download, which aggregates records from multiple contributing networks, primarily eBird \citep{sullivan2009ebird}, iNaturalist, and observation.org. Because GBIF provides single occurrence records rather than structured checklists, the data is presence-only: each record attests that a species was observed at a coordinate and date, but says nothing about which other species were or were not detected. Records are filtered to species present in the project taxonomy, restricted to binomial names with valid coordinates and dates, and mapped to H3 cells.

Within each cell, observations are aggregated into 48 weekly species lists by taking the set union of all species recorded during that week. The pipeline retains only the day and month of each record, so observations from every year in the archive are pooled into a single annual cycle and the model represents a multi-year climatology rather than the state of any particular year. The archive spans several decades, but its records are distributed very unevenly over that period: the bulk of them date from roughly the past fifteen years, following the wide adoption of eBird and iNaturalist, and the annual volume continues to grow. Because labels are set unions, a species recorded once contributes exactly as much as one recorded ten thousand times, so the growth of the observation record over time cannot inflate any label. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} discusses the narrower channels through which it still acts.

Environmental features (elevation, precipitation, temperature, land-cover fractions, and other bioclimatic variables) are sampled independently from Google Earth Engine \citep{crego2022implementation} at each H3 cell centroid and joined to the occurrence data. For training, each cell is flattened into 48 weekly samples whose spatial input is the cell center coordinate. Coordinate jitter, Gaussian noise scaled to the cell radius, provides spatial augmentation that discourages overfitting to exact cell centers.

The resulting dataset spans 287,525 H3 cells containing approximately 70.9 million species-level observations. Observation density per cell spans four orders of magnitude and is strongly biased toward North America (29.1M observations in 42.3K cells, about 688 obs/cell) and Europe (11.8M in 11.3K cells, about 1,046 obs/cell), which hold 40.9\,M of the 70.9\,M observations between them. The remainder is spread over South America (10.6M in 19.3K cells, about 552 obs/cell), Asia (10.1M in 59.0K cells, about 170 obs/cell), Africa (4.9M in 32.0K cells, about 152 obs/cell), and Oceania (4.2M in 16.7K cells, about 254 obs/cell), and large parts of Africa, central Asia, and Amazonia carry a handful of records per cell.

\subsubsection{Feature Encoding}
\label{sec:features}

Environmental features are encoded type-appropriately: categorical variables such as land-cover class are one-hot encoded, fractional variables such as water cover are passed through directly, and continuous variables such as elevation and precipitation are z-score standardized. Missing values are masked so that the loss function ignores cells where a given feature is unavailable (Section~\ref{sec:loss}). Ocean-dominated cells can be downsampled to reduce their influence on training.

Species are identified by eBird codes for birds and iNaturalist taxon IDs for other taxa. With vocabularies exceeding 10,000 species, labels are stored in a sparse format and expanded to full multi-label vectors during batching to keep memory usage tractable. Species observed fewer than a minimum number of times are excluded from the vocabulary.

\subsubsection{Label Propagation}
\label{sec:propagation}

Many H3 cells, particularly outside North America and Europe, contain too few observations to provide useful training signal, yet the species present in those regions are not absent, only unrecorded. Label propagation fills these gaps by assigning pseudo-labels from environmentally similar, well-observed cells. For each under-sampled cell the $k$ nearest neighbors in standardized environmental feature space are retrieved, subject to several ecological constraints. Neighbors must share the same week, which prevents seasonal leakage; lie within a geographic radius cap, which prevents ecologically implausible long-range transfer; and fall below an environmental-distance threshold in standardized feature space, which rejects geographically close but ecologically dissimilar neighbors. In addition, each species may propagate only within a distance proportional to its observed range diameter from the nearest original observation, subject to a hard kilometer ceiling, which prevents cosmopolitan species from filling in distant cells. Algorithm~\ref{alg:propagation} gives the procedure.

A cell counts as under-sampled when its recorded species list is shorter than a threshold: the sparsity criterion is a species count, not a raw record count. The six parameters governing propagation are jointly optimized via Bayesian search (Section~\ref{sec:search}).


\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}\small\begin{verbatim}
Input: samples S = {(lat, lon, week, species_list, env_features)}
Parameters: k, max_radius, min_obs, max_spread, env_dist_max, range_cap

1  Partition samples into OBSERVED (|species_list| >= min_obs) and SPARSE
2  Standardize environmental features across all samples (zero mean, unit variance)
3  For each species s in OBSERVED samples:
4      Compute bounding-box diagonal d_s from all observations of s
5      Set max_propagation_dist_s ← min(max_spread x d_s / 2, range_cap)
6      Build a spatial index over all original observation locations of s
7  For each week w:
8      Build a KD-tree over env features of OBSERVED samples in week w
9      For each SPARSE sample q in week w:
10         Query k nearest OBSERVED neighbors in env space
11         For each neighbor n:
12             If geographic_distance(q, n) > max_radius: discard n
13             If env_distance(q, n) > env_dist_max: discard n
14         Collect candidate species from all remaining neighbors
15         For each candidate species s:
16             d ← distance from q to nearest original observation of s
17             If d > max_propagation_dist_s: discard s
18         Add surviving species to q's species list
\end{verbatim}\end{minipage}
\caption{Label propagation. Species lists are copied from well-observed cells to environmentally similar sparse cells, subject to five constraints: same week, geographic radius cap, environmental-distance gate, per-species range cap, and a kilometre ceiling on transfer distance.}
\label{alg:propagation}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Frequency-Based Label Weights}
\label{sec:freq-weights}

Citizen-science observation density varies by orders of magnitude across regions. Naive global weighting would upweight common species in data-rich areas while suppressing species-rich tropical communities. We therefore compute region-normalized frequency weights: samples are partitioned into coarse geographic bins, species frequency percentiles are computed within each bin, and the maximum percentile across bins determines the final weight. A species common in Colombia is then penalized like one common in the US, decoupling label weighting from raw observation volume. Weights are derived from pre-propagation species lists to avoid contamination by pseudo-labels.

\subsection{Loss Functions}
\label{sec:loss}

The label matrix is extremely sparse: a typical location hosts tens to low hundreds of species out of a vocabulary exceeding 10,000, so more than 99\% of labels are zero. The zeros are also unreliable: a species scored as absent may simply be unrecorded, especially in under-surveyed regions. A loss here must handle severe class imbalance and noisy negatives at once. We implement four loss functions spanning progressively stronger assumptions and compare them empirically in Section~\ref{sec:res-ablation}.

The total training objective is a weighted sum of species and environmental terms (plus a habitat term when the optional head of Appendix~\ref{sec:E-refuted-design-choices} is active):

\begin{equation*}
\mathcal{L}_\text{total} = w_s \cdot \mathcal{L}_\text{species} + w_e \cdot \mathcal{L}_\text{env} + w_h \cdot \mathcal{L}_\text{habitat}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Binary cross-entropy (BCE)} is the standard multi-label baseline. It treats every zero as a true absence and every one as a true presence, applying equal gradient pressure to all species regardless of how confident the prediction already is. Its uniform gradient avoids the calibration distortions that more aggressive reweighting schemes introduce, and label propagation has already filled in many of the false zeros before the loss sees them.

\textbf{Focal loss} \citep{lin2017focal} modulates the per-example gradient by a factor $(1 - p_t)^\gamma$, where $p_t$ is the predicted probability for the correct class, so that well-classified examples contribute almost no gradient and learning concentrates on hard cases. An additional per-class weight $\alpha$ balances positive and negative contributions. Focal loss was designed for dense object detection, where the ratio of background to foreground is similarly extreme; here it serves the analogous purpose of preventing the overwhelming majority of zero labels from drowning out the signal from present species.

\textbf{Asymmetric loss} (ASL; \citealp{ridnik2021asymmetric}) extends the focal idea by applying separate focusing parameters to positive ($\gamma_+$) and negative ($\gamma_-$) terms, on the reasoning that the two error types have different costs: a false negative is unrecoverable when the model is used as a filter, whereas a false positive is not. ASL also introduces a probability margin $m$ that shifts the negative probability down before the loss is computed:

\begin{equation*}
\mathcal{L}_\text{ASL} = -\bigl[y \cdot (1-p)^{\gamma_+} \log p + (1-y) \cdot p_m^{\gamma_-} \log(1-p_m)\bigr], \quad p_m = \max(p - m,\; 0)
\end{equation*}

When $p < m$ the negative term vanishes entirely, so the model pays no penalty for predicting a low probability for an absent species once it is already below the margin.

\textbf{Assume-Negative (AN) loss} \citep{cole2023spatial} is the only one of the four that explicitly acknowledges presence-only data. It implements the LAN-full (Location-Aware Assume Negative) strategy from SINR: at each observed location, every species not recorded in the checklist is assumed absent. Some of those species are genuinely present but went undetected, so positive observations are up-weighted by a factor $\lambda$ (default 4) to compensate. For computational efficiency with a vocabulary of roughly 12K species, only a random subset of negative species is evaluated per sample, and the sampled loss is rescaled to provide an unbiased approximation of the full-vocabulary expectation. Optional label smoothing softens the binary targets slightly.

\textbf{Environmental loss.} The environmental head is trained with masked mean squared error, computed only over cells where the Earth Engine target is available. The environmental weight $w_e$ controls how strongly the regression objective shapes the shared encoder representation: set too high, it biases the encoder toward environmental reconstruction at the expense of species discrimination; set to zero, it removes the auxiliary signal entirely and leaves the encoder to discover environmental structure from species co-occurrence alone.

\subsection{Training Procedure}
\label{sec:training}

The model is trained with AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019decoupled} using decoupled weight decay. The learning rate follows a cosine annealing schedule with a three-epoch linear warmup, and gradient norms are clipped to prevent training instabilities. Training uses automatic mixed precision on CUDA GPUs: forward passes run in FP16 for roughly double the throughput, while loss computation and parameter updates remain in full precision.

\textbf{Location-based splitting.} To prevent spatial data leakage, a concern raised by \citet{kellenberger2026performance}, the train/validation split operates at the level of H3 cells rather than individual samples. All 48 weekly samples from a given cell go entirely into either the training or the validation set, so the model is always evaluated on locations it has never seen. Entire well-surveyed geographic regions can additionally be withheld from training for holdout evaluation; we define five such regions (US Northwest, BeNeLux, Great Britain, California, and Japan), chosen for high eBird observer density and therefore reliable ground-truth labels.

\textbf{Early stopping.} Training halts when the composite GeoScore metric (Section~\ref{sec:geoscore}) does not improve for a configurable number of epochs. The best-scoring checkpoint is retained.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Search}
\label{sec:search}

An automated Bayesian search \citep{akiba2019optuna} optimizes GeoScore over up to 23 parameters spanning loss function selection and tuning, model capacity (scale factor, harmonic counts), task weighting, data augmentation, frequency weighting, and label propagation; Appendix~\ref{sec:D-hyperparameter-search-space} lists the full space. The search uses TPE sampling with median pruning for early termination of unpromising trials. Selective tuning, in which some parameters are fixed while others are searched over, allows efficient exploration of individual design dimensions and is what the staged ablation of Section~\ref{sec:res-ablation} exploits.

\subsection{Export and Deployment}
\label{sec:export}

Trained models are exported to ONNX, TensorFlow Lite (FP32, FP16, and INT8 dynamic-range quantization), and TensorFlow SavedModel. The critical format is FP16, which compresses the production model (roughly 12K species, scale 0.75) to approximately 7\,MB, within the 10\,MB budget for mobile apps and embedded acoustic sensors; INT8 dynamic-range quantization reduces this further to about 4\,MB. Inputs and outputs retain full precision even in the FP16 model, preserving coordinate and probability accuracy. After each conversion, an automated validation compares outputs against the PyTorch reference across diverse locations and weeks and confirms negligible differences. The bounded FiLM gamma and conservative LayerNorm epsilon of Section~\ref{sec:architecture} are what make that agreement possible.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:evaluation}

A model of this kind must rank species correctly at well-surveyed locations, generalize to poorly observed regions, and stay calibrated: a model that assigns high probability to every species achieves perfect recall and is useless as a filter. We therefore evaluate along three axes, combined into a single composite metric that serves as the optimization target during training and hyperparameter search.

\subsubsection{Component Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

\textbf{Ranking.} Mean average precision (mAP) is computed per sample: for each validation location $\times$ week, all $S$ species are ranked by predicted probability and the average precision of the true positive set is averaged across samples. Top-$k$ recall at $k = 10$ and $k = 30$ measures the fraction of truly present species appearing in the model's $k$ highest-scoring predictions.

\textbf{Calibration.} At probability thresholds of 5\%, 10\%, and 25\% we compute micro-averaged precision, recall, and F1. The 10\% threshold is the primary operating point, being the threshold at which the BirdNET app and related tools decide whether a species is plausible at a given location and week. We also report the mean list length (species exceeding the threshold per sample) and the list ratio (predicted list length divided by true list length), which reveals whether the model over- or under-predicts species counts.

\textbf{Observation bias.} A model that memorizes observation density, predicting many species in New York and few in Amazonia, would achieve respectable mAP while having learned nothing about ecology. We measure susceptibility to this in two ways. Validation samples are split into quartiles by the observation density of their H3 cell, where density is the summed length of the recorded species lists at that location and serves as a survey-effort proxy; we report mAP separately for the sparsest and densest quartiles and their ratio. Separately, we compute the Pearson correlation between observation density and the number of species predicted above the 10\% threshold. A high positive correlation is the signature of a model that has absorbed sampling bias rather than filtered it out.

\textbf{Rare species.} To ensure aggregate metrics do not mask failures on conservation-relevant taxa, we track per-species average precision for a curated watchlist of 18 endemic and restricted-range species drawn from Hawaii, New Zealand, and the Galápagos, plus Kagu, California Condor, and Whooping Crane. Appendix~\ref{sec:C-watchlist-species} lists them. Watchlist AP is the unweighted mean across watchlist species with at least one positive sample in the validation set.

\subsubsection{GeoScore}
\label{sec:geoscore}

GeoScore is a weighted average of the seven components in Table~\ref{tab:geoscore}, each normalized to $[0, 1]$ where higher is better. It penalizes a model that excels on one axis, such as raw ranking, while failing on another, such as predicting far too many species per location. Components that are unavailable, for instance holdout mAP when no holdout regions are defined, are dropped and the remaining weights renormalized, which keeps GeoScore comparable across runs with different evaluation configurations. Ranking quality receives 40\% of the weight and bias robustness 25\%, list calibration 15\%, and the remaining 20\% is split between geographic generalization and rare-species coverage. These weights were set before the ablation study and held fixed throughout. Appendix~\ref{sec:A-geoscore-component-definitions} gives the exact transformations.

\subsubsection{Reference Comparisons}
\label{sec:protocols}

Beyond the internal validation split, we evaluate the production model against two external references.

\textbf{eBird Status and Trends.} We compare occurrence predictions against S\&T 2023 median abundance surfaces \citep{fink2024ebird} for 10 species spanning diverse migration strategies and geographic extents: long-distance migrants (Amur Falcon, Bobolink, Common Swift, Northern Wheatear), medium-distance migrants (European Bee-eater, Ruby-throated Hummingbird, Rufous Hummingbird, Wilson's Warbler), and residents (Blue-and-yellow Macaw, Northern Cardinal). Each species is evaluated at week 1 (early January) and week 26 (mid-July). Both surfaces are projected onto a shared 0.5\textdegree{} global grid (360 $\times$ 720 = 259,200 cells). Because S\&T outputs relative abundance while our model outputs occurrence probability, direct value comparison is not meaningful, so both are binarized: S\&T cells with any positive abundance count as present, and geomodel predictions are thresholded at a single global value chosen to maximize mean F1 across all 20 species-week pairs. That optimum is 0.167; we round down to $t = 0.15$, within 0.3\% of optimal mean F1, because a round number is easier to communicate and apply. All metrics are computed within the S\&T valid mask, so the geomodel is not penalized for predicting in regions S\&T does not cover. We report Jaccard index, precision, recall, F1, and AUC, the last computed from the geomodel's continuous probability against S\&T binary presence.

\textbf{BirdNET app submissions.} The second comparison evaluates the deployed configuration, in which the geomodel is applied after the acoustic classifier and asked only whether a species is plausible at a given location and week. From the 39.6\,M app submissions recorded in 2025, each a short recording with a location, a timestamp, and the on-device classifier's top detections \citep{wood2022machine}, we retain those with a confident on-device detection (score > 0.5), no human vocalization among the ten reported classes, a duration of 2.5--3.5\,s so that a single centered 3\,s window covers the recording, and valid coordinates and date. This yields 1,718,002 candidates, of which 83.7\% are European. We subsample with an equal quota per occupied H3 resolution-2 cell (about 86,000\,km$^2$), rotating over cells and over devices within each cell and redistributing the quota of exhausted cells, giving 100,032 recordings across 1,216 cells with the European share reduced to 39.0\%. Each recording's centered 3\,s window is classified with the BirdNET+ V3.0 developer preview (\texttt{V3.0-preview3.1\_Global\_11K}, 11,560 classes, 32\,kHz input; \citealp{lasseck2026birdnetv3}). For every top-3 prediction of class \emph{Aves} we query the geomodel at the recording's latitude, longitude, and week and mark the prediction rejected if its occurrence probability falls below the same $t = 0.15$. Classes are matched by scientific name; 99.37\% of bird predictions have a geomodel counterpart, and the remainder are excluded rather than counted as rejections. Appendix~\ref{sec:F-post-filter-study-selection-and-sampling} gives the full selection and sampling detail.

The quantity this second comparison measures is \emph{selectivity}: how much of the candidate list the filter removes, and at which ranks and confidence levels. Whether the underlying identifications are correct is not measured, because these submissions carry no verified identifications.


\section{Results}

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:res-ablation}

The ablation comprises 46 grid-search experiments (Stages A--G) plus 60 Bayesian propagation-parameter optimization trials (Stage H). Each grid stage isolates one design dimension, selects a single winner by GeoScore, and carries that configuration forward. This greedy, sequential protocol is more sample-efficient than full factorial search while still quantifying the marginal contribution of each factor. All experiments share a common base configuration: batch size 1024, 20 training epochs, AdamW with cosine-annealed learning rate ($10^{-3}$), patience-5 early stopping on GeoScore, location-based split, no yearly encoding, and a minimum species-observation threshold of 50. Holdout regions were not used during ablation, so the holdout mAP component of GeoScore is absent and the remaining weights are renormalized. We report the best-epoch GeoScore for each run. Table~\ref{tab:stages} summarizes the stage winners; full per-run tables are in Appendix~\ref{sec:B-full-ablation-results-tables}.

\textbf{Loss family (Stage A, 12 runs).} Three settings per family (conservative, default, aggressive) were tested at reference model scale with a moderate environmental task weight. All 12 runs achieve nearly identical mean average precision (0.536--0.544), while calibration varies by orders of magnitude. BCE produces a list ratio at the 10\% threshold of approximately 6. ASL down-weights easy negatives so aggressively that predicted lists inflate instead: its most aggressive setting yields LR@10\% = 979, at which point the model predicts nearly every species at every location. Focal loss shows the same pattern at reduced severity. AN loss is the second-best family and trails BCE by roughly 11\% in GeoScore. BCE with light label smoothing (0.05) achieves the highest GeoScore (0.422) and a watchlist AP of 0.701.

\textbf{Capacity and representation mode (Stage B, 9 runs).} Three model scale factors (0.5$\times$, 1.0$\times$, 2.0$\times$) were crossed with three representation modes: coordinate-only, environmental auxiliary regression, and environment plus habitat head. Model scale dominates both representation choices. GeoScore, mAP, and F1@10\% improve monotonically from the smallest to the largest model within every mode (GeoScore 0.408 $\rightarrow$ 0.431 for coordinate-only), and coordinate-only wins at scale 2.0. At the largest scale the environmental mode achieves slightly higher raw mAP (0.552 versus 0.547) at the cost of worse calibration, netting a lower GeoScore. The habitat head improves no metric at any scale (Appendix~\ref{sec:E-refuted-design-choices}). Watchlist AP is the one metric that does not track scale monotonically --- it fluctuates within the environmental and habitat modes --- but the largest coordinate-only model still achieves the best endemic-species performance in the stage (0.730).

\textbf{Observation bias and label propagation (Stage C, 8 runs).} Two mechanisms were tested against observation bias: the environmental auxiliary task at four weight levels, and label propagation. Enabling propagation raises GeoScore from approximately 0.43 to 0.56, a 31\% improvement. The gain is a structural redistribution: mAP in the sparsest observation-density quartile rises from 0.28 to 0.87 (+210\%) while mAP in the densest quartile falls from 0.85 to 0.72 ($-$15\%), and the density ratio moves from 0.33 to 1.21. Watchlist AP drops from 0.724 to 0.638 over the same change, as propagated pseudo-labels blur the boundaries of highly range-restricted species. The environmental auxiliary task has no measurable effect: across all four weight levels GeoScore varies by less than 1\% in both propagation regimes.

\textbf{Encoding, augmentation, and data interventions (Stages D--G, 17 runs).} All six harmonic configurations span 0.004 in GeoScore (0.562--0.566), with the maximum setting (eight harmonics each for coordinates and week) winning narrowly. Coordinate jitter and yearly encoding produce differences below 0.3\%; jitter is marginally positive (+0.1\% GeoScore) and yearly encoding consistently negative. Capping observations per species is destructive: a cap at 5000 cuts GeoScore from 0.566 to 0.388 as species discrimination collapses (mAP 0.697 $\rightarrow$ 0.207), a cap at 50,000 preserves reasonable mAP (0.521) with a modest GeoScore penalty (0.457), and a cap at 500 collapses entirely (GeoScore 0.092). Under capping the density ratio improves to 1.78, past the ideal balance of 1.0, which GeoScore penalizes symmetrically. Vocabulary scaling within the capped regime shows GeoScore at 5000 species (0.434) exceeding the full vocabulary at 11,827 species (0.388), with mAP decreasing monotonically from 0.879 at 10 species to 0.207 at 11,827.

\textbf{Propagation parameters (Stage H, 60 trials).} Stage H optimizes six propagation parameters. Beyond the three used in earlier stages (neighbor count, search radius, sparsity threshold), it introduces an environmental-distance gate, a per-species range cap measured from the nearest original observation, and a range-spread factor scaling each species' observed range diameter. An initial exploratory search exposed a failure mode of the objective: without explicit constraints the optimizer drove the search radius to 4,946\,km, against a 5,000\,km ceiling, and the range-spread factor to its upper bound. More propagation raises mAP, F1, and list calibration monotonically, and in that first search the density-ratio and watchlist components that would have counterbalanced them were not recorded and so dropped out of the renormalized GeoScore, leaving nothing to penalize ecologically implausible transfer. Restoring those components and bounding expansion by distance from the nearest true observation removes the behavior. The optimized configuration ($k$ = 20, max radius 976\,km, sparsity threshold 12, max spread 0.93, environmental gate 4.99, range cap 1471\,km) yields the best discrimination among the constrained configurations (mAP 0.781) together with a near-balanced density ratio of 0.973 and the highest GeoScore (0.638). Watchlist AP under this configuration is 0.535.

\textbf{Production configuration.} The ablation favors the largest scale tested (2.0), but at roughly 47M parameters it would occupy about 94\,MB in FP16, and scale 1.0 produces a 14\,MB FP16 model that still misses the 10\,MB deployment target. Scale 0.75 (3.8M parameters, about 7\,MB in FP16) retains most of the capacity benefit of scale 1.0 while fitting comfortably on mobile phones and embedded sensors, and is the configuration used for the two reference comparisons that follow. The environmental auxiliary task is retained during training at negligible cost.

\subsection{Comparison with eBird Status and Trends}
\label{sec:res-ebirdst}

Across the 20 species--week pairs, discrimination is uniformly strong: mean AUC is 0.975, and only two pairs fall below 0.95, Common Swift in July (0.943) and Northern Wheatear in July (0.880). Threshold-dependent overlap varies more widely, from 0.199 to 0.795 in Jaccard, with a mean of 0.632 and a mean F1 of 0.764. At $t = 0.15$ mean precision is 0.683 and mean recall is 0.910, and recall exceeds precision in 15 of 20 comparisons: the model claims presence in cells where S\&T does not, predominantly at range margins, rather than missing core range. Table~\ref{tab:ebirdst} in Appendix~\ref{sec:H-ebirdst-full-results} gives all five metrics per species and week.

Measured precision should be read as a lower bound rather than an estimate. S\&T publishes nothing outside its modeled regions, and within them it does not assert presence in cells whose sampling cannot support the claim, so a cell we mark present and S\&T does not is ambiguous between our false positive and their abstention.

European Bee-eater achieves the highest overlap in both seasons (Jaccard 0.795 and 0.772) and Common Swift is strong in both (0.764, 0.767). Blue-and-yellow Macaw, a tropical resident, is stable across seasons (0.690, 0.720). For several migrants, particularly both hummingbirds and Wilson's Warbler, January Jaccard is substantially lower than July, driven by lower precision. Rufous Hummingbird in January is the weakest pair (Jaccard 0.199, F1 0.331), where the model over-extends the species' narrow wintering corridor in southern Mexico. Seasonal reversals are reproduced: Common Swift shifts from sub-Saharan Africa in January to the Palearctic in July, Bobolink between South and North America, and Amur Falcon from southern Africa to central Asia (Figure~\ref{fig:rangemaps}).

\subsection{Post-Filter Study on BirdNET App Submissions}
\label{sec:res-filter}

At $t = 0.15$ the geomodel rejects 58,396 of 296,251 bird predictions across the study set (19.7\%). Table~\ref{tab:filter} breaks that rate down by prediction rank and classifier confidence. The filter removes 25.7\% and 28.9\% of second- and third-ranked candidates but 4.8\% of top-1 predictions and 2.1\% of predictions above 0.90 confidence. Above a confidence of 0.25 the rejection rate declines monotonically and steeply (Figure~\ref{fig:filter}a). Per recording, 2.38 of 2.96 scored bird candidates survive; 41.1\% of recordings lose at least one candidate and 2.0\% lose every scored candidate, the case in which a post-filter would return an empty candidate list.

Between $t = 0.02$ and $t = 0.20$ the rank-1 rejection rate moves only from 2.5\% to 5.8\% while rank-2/3 rejections stay above 20\% throughout, because the geomodel's scores for top-1 predictions are strongly bimodal (median 0.843). Only beyond $t = 0.3$ does the cost to top-1 predictions rise sharply, reaching 16.3\% at $t = 0.5$.

Rejection rates vary strongly by region: 11.2\% in North America and 14.5\% in Europe against 34.6\% in Asia and 46.4\% in Africa (Figure~\ref{fig:filter}d; per-region counts in Appendix~\ref{sec:F-post-filter-study-selection-and-sampling}).

The seven species removed most frequently are all ones a global classifier is expected to over-predict outside their ranges: Northern Cardinal (2,974 rejections, almost all as low-scoring rank-2/3 candidates), Common Blackbird (1,007, predominantly in Asia and Africa), Italian Sparrow (927), American Robin (736, predominantly in Europe and Asia), Eurasian Chaffinch (685, predominantly in Africa), African Chaffinch (597, predominantly in Europe), and Red-winged Blackbird (489). The two chaffinches are the mirror-image case: a confusable species pair whose members the classifier proposes on each other's continent. Of the 839 North American cardinal rejections, 80.2\% lie west of 100\textdegree{}\,W or north of 50\textdegree{}\,N.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{A Filter, Not a Full SDM}
\label{sec:disc-filter}

BirdNET Geomodel is not meant to replace curated range maps, effort-corrected abundance surfaces, or independently validated SDMs. Its role is narrower: given a location and a week, narrow a vocabulary of over 10,000 species down to the ecologically plausible subset, so that an audio classifier does not spend probability mass on species that could not realistically be present. A Common Myna flagged in Minnesota, or a European Bee-eater in Brazil, is noise, and suppressing it is the whole job.

That framing sets the evaluation bar. A filtering model must reliably include species that are present and not grossly over-include species that are absent; it does not need the accuracy of a published range map, nor to distinguish fine-scale habitat within a 26\,km H3 cell. GeoScore encodes those priorities directly, weighting discrimination and spatial fairness above calibration and rare-species coverage.

The two reference comparisons measure different properties of one model, and the contrast between them is the main empirical result of this paper. Scored as a range map against eBird S\&T, the probability surfaces look unremarkable: a mean Jaccard of 0.632 is a moderate overlap. Applied in the deployed configuration, the same surfaces leave 95.2\% of the classifier's primary identifications and 97.9\% of its high-confidence predictions untouched while removing roughly a quarter of the low-ranked alternatives. The over-inclusiveness visible in the first comparison, recall 0.910 against precision 0.683, is what makes the second possible. A variant tuned to maximize Jaccard would plausibly be a worse filter, because precision gained at range margins is paid for in recall exactly where vagrants and range-edge records occur. The model's known over-extension into coastal waters follows the same logic, making the filter more permissive near coasts rather than less.

The deployment constraints follow from the same framing. A heavier model that marginally improves watchlist AP on island endemics would not be a useful trade against the 10\,MB budget, and querying elevation databases or land-cover APIs at detection time is impractical in the field. The 48-week temporal resolution is coarser than the 52-week S\&T calendar but fine enough that no species shifts from present to absent within a single model-week under realistic conditions.

\subsection{The Primacy of Label Propagation}
\label{sec:disc-propagation}

Label propagation accounts for the two largest GeoScore gains in the ablation, enabling it and then constraining and tuning it, for a cumulative 48\% improvement over the pre-propagation baseline. It works as spatial data augmentation applied to the targets rather than the inputs: copying species lists from observed cells to environmentally similar unobserved cells forces the model to learn distributions in regions where it would otherwise receive no training signal at all. That is why the gain takes the form of a redistribution rather than a uniform lift, with sparse-quartile mAP rising and dense-quartile mAP falling slightly.

The ecological constraints are what keep this honest. Without them the optimizer finds that GeoScore rewards aggressive propagation and pushes radii to the 5,000\,km ceiling of the search space, producing range expansions with no ecological basis; the environmental-distance gate and the per-species range cap redirect it toward plausible cells. The cost is borne by range-restricted species, whose watchlist AP falls from 0.635 to 0.535 as crisp boundaries blur. For a filter that is the acceptable direction of error, and arguably the acceptable population to lose accuracy on, since a species endemic to a single island will not appear in most audio submissions regardless of where the model's confidence boundary sits. It remains the clearest cost of the approach (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Calibration, Scale, and Architecture}
\label{sec:disc-calibration}

The loss-function result is the one we did not expect. All four families rank species almost identically, and what separates them is calibration, which varies by two orders of magnitude. Focal-style down-weighting suppresses easy negatives so effectively that the model begins assigning high probability to nearly everything, which is fatal for a filter operating at a fixed threshold. BCE with light label smoothing avoids that failure mode and is the practical choice for anyone building a similar system.

Model scale gives a consistent but modest return: 5.5\% GeoScore from 0.5 to 2.0 in coordinate-only mode, split evenly between the two doublings. Because the deployment budget binds long before the ablation optimum, scale 0.75 is the production point, and the gap to scale 2.0 is smaller than the gap between having and not having constrained propagation.

The environmental auxiliary task provides no benefit to species prediction. Once the model is large enough, the mapping from coordinates to species occurrence already captures the environmental gradients that matter: elevation, climate, and land cover vary smoothly across space, and a multi-harmonic coordinate encoding has ample capacity to approximate them without being told they exist. We retain the task because it costs nothing measurable and its intermediate representations may be useful for downstream habitat or abundance work. The habitat head is not recommended.

\subsection{Operating the Filter in Practice}
\label{sec:disc-practice}

Threshold selection is not critical. Across the range $t = 0.02$ to $t = 0.20$ the rank-1 rejection rate moves by roughly three percentage points while rank-2/3 rejections stay above 20\%, because the geomodel's scores for top-1 predictions are strongly bimodal. The consequential decision is whether to filter at all, not where exactly to place the boundary, and $t = 0.15$ inherited from the S\&T comparison is a defensible default that the submission data give no reason to revise.

The filter acts predominantly where the classifier is uncertain. Rejection rates fall monotonically above a classifier confidence of 0.25 and reach 2.1\% in the top bucket; above 0.75 the filter is close to inactive. A confidence-gated variant that applies the geomodel only to the contested middle of the score range would forfeit few rejections while eliminating most of the residual risk to confident predictions. That configuration is preferable wherever false suppression is more costly than a spurious candidate, and it is the most direct extension of this work.

The intervention rate is strongly region-dependent, three to four times higher in Africa and Asia than in North America. Two mechanisms push in the same direction and this study design cannot separate them: the acoustic classifier makes more out-of-range errors where its training data is sparse, and the geomodel's occurrence surfaces are least reliable in the same regions. Under either explanation a globally uniform threshold makes the filter intervene most strongly where both models are least reliable, which is the opposite of what a risk-averse configuration would do. Region-aware thresholds, or thresholds conditioned on local propagation quality, merit investigation before the filter is relied upon for automated data products in these regions; the confidence gate above is the mitigation available without further work.

\subsection{Relation to Existing Approaches}
\label{sec:disc-related}

The model inherits SINR's coordinate-only paradigm \citep{cole2023spatial} and adds weekly temporal resolution through FiLM conditioning, producing 48 occurrence surfaces per species rather than one static range map. eBird Status and Trends \citep{fink2024ebird} also operates at weekly resolution but models each species independently with effort covariates and adaptive ensembles, achieving greater ecological precision at the cost of being unsuitable for on-device deployment across more than 10,000 species. The finding of \citet{kellenberger2026performance} that deep-learning models match but do not surpass classical SDMs, and underperform for threatened species, is a useful caution: our watchlist component and density-stratified evaluation are designed to surface exactly those failure modes, even though closing them is outside the scope of a filtering model.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Presence-only data and pseudo-labels.} Absences in our data reflect non-detection, not verified absence, and label propagation fills the resulting gaps with approximations rather than ground truth. The prediction--density correlation remains high at roughly 0.87, so the model still predicts richer communities in heavily surveyed areas; the near-optimal density ratio shows this bias is balanced across quartiles, but the absolute coupling to observer effort has not been eliminated.

\textbf{The model is a climatology.} Occurrence records are pooled across all observation years and collapsed onto a 48-week annual cycle, so the model cannot track real-time range shifts, invasive species spread, or year-to-year population change. Because per-cell labels are set unions, the growth of the observation record over time cannot inflate any individual label, but it still acts through three narrower channels: cells and weeks that only became sampled recently exist as training samples at all because of that growth; the count-based thresholds for vocabulary inclusion and for propagation donor eligibility favor recently well-surveyed cells; and the frequency weights are computed over observation counts dominated by recent years. Because the record is so heavily weighted toward the past decade and a half, the resulting climatology sits close to present-day conditions, which suits the filtering application but makes the model a poor baseline against which to measure change. Quantifying these effects would require a year-aware variant trained with year-level labels, which the current pipeline does not retain.

\textbf{Neither reference comparison is independent.} Both our model and eBird S\&T derive from eBird checklists, so agreement partly reflects a shared data source and disagreement is the more informative signal. S\&T also withholds predictions where observations are sparse, which is exactly where a coordinate-only model extrapolates most and is least checkable, and within its mask it reports thinly sampled cells as absent rather than uncertain. Both effects push measured precision down without evidence that the geomodel is wrong. Validation against expert-curated range polygons, for example BirdLife International, would give a genuinely independent spatial accuracy assessment. The 0.5\textdegree{} comparison grid, roughly 55\,km at the equator, additionally smooths fine-scale range boundaries.

\textbf{The post-filter study measures selectivity, not correctness.} These submissions carry no verified identifications, so the 4.8\% of rejected top-1 predictions cannot be separated into correctly caught out-of-range errors and wrongly suppressed rarities. Vagrants and range-edge records are by construction concentrated in the rejected set, and they are the one population a filter of this kind can harm and the one this evaluation cannot see. A labeled follow-up using the app's explicit user-confirmation field would convert that rejection rate into a genuine false-suppression rate. The selection criteria, a confident on-device detection and a 2.5--3.5\,s duration, also favor short, clean recordings, so rejection rates on the unfiltered submission stream would likely be higher than those reported here.


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

BirdNET Geomodel predicts weekly occurrence probabilities for over 10,000 species from raw geographic coordinates and week of year, with no environmental covariates at inference. A single shared network produces 48 occurrence surfaces per species and compresses global spatiotemporal species distributions into approximately 7\,MB in FP16, replacing hundreds of megabytes of raw observation data and enabling real-time species filtering on mobile phones and embedded acoustic sensors.

The ablation yields three findings. Ecologically constrained label propagation, imputing species lists from well-observed cells to environmentally similar unobserved ones, matters more than the choice of loss function or any architectural variant we tested, and is the only intervention that substantially improves performance in under-surveyed regions. Loss function choice affects probability calibration far more than species ranking, which makes BCE with light label smoothing the default for filtering applications. The model learns environmental gradients implicitly from species co-occurrence, so explicit environmental auxiliary tasks add no measurable benefit to species prediction.

Both reference comparisons support the filter framing. Against eBird Status and Trends the model reproduces range geometry and seasonal migration without environmental inputs, and its dominant error is over-prediction in data-sparse wintering grounds, which is the benign direction for filtering. Applied as a post-filter to the acoustic classifier's top-3 predictions on 100,032 globally balanced app submissions, it removes roughly a quarter of the second- and third-ranked candidates while leaving the large majority of primary and high-confidence identifications untouched. Rejection rates three to four times higher in Africa and Asia than in North America argue for confidence-gated or region-aware thresholds before the filter is relied on for automated data products in under-surveyed regions.

Several directions warrant investigation. Extending the model to capture inter-annual range shifts from climate and land-use change would require multi-year temporal encoding and training data with year-level labels. Finer spatial resolution (H3 level 5 or 6) could improve predictions near range boundaries but would demand substantially more training data. Targeted enrichment of observation data in under-surveyed tropical and marine regions would directly benefit propagation quality. Finally, the environmental embeddings learned by the auxiliary task offer a starting point for abundance prediction and habitat classification without additional environmental inputs at inference.
